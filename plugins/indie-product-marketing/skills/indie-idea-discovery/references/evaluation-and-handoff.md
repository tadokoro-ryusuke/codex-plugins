# Opportunity Evaluation and Handoff

Use this reference to reduce a diverse candidate set into a small, honest portfolio and transfer selected opportunities into demand validation.

## Contents

1. Prepare candidates
2. Apply hard gates
3. Score evidence and attractiveness
4. Build a portfolio
5. Choose the next learning action
6. Decide pursue, research, park, or reject
7. Hand off to marketing

## 1. Prepare candidates

Do not score raw slogans. Create a minimum opportunity card containing:

- Actor and triggering situation.
- Desired progress and consequence.
- Current substitute and switching barrier.
- Evidence items and grades.
- Proposed promise and mechanism.
- Beneficiary and payer.
- Founder access or insight.
- Acquisition premise.
- Recurring value or intentionally one-time economics.
- Operating and safety constraints.
- Riskiest assumptions.

Keep unknown fields marked **U**. Missing evidence must remain visible.

## 2. Apply hard gates

Evaluate gates before totals.

| Gate | Pass condition | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Learnability | The problem can be investigated ethically within the founder's budget and reach | Anonymous audience with no credible access path |
| Deliverability | The founder can produce a safe next-value artifact | Requires production-grade regulated automation immediately |
| Integrity | The product does not depend on deception, prohibited data, or harmful claims | Fabricated users, fake engagement, unlicensed sensitive data |
| Consequence | Evidence or a feasible research path suggests meaningful customer progress | Pure novelty with no consequence |
| Value logic | Recurring value, a payment event, strategic learning, or a bounded non-commercial objective exists | No reason for use or learning after the first interaction |

Classify each gate:

- **Pass** — adequate evidence for the current decision.
- **Conditional** — specific missing evidence can be acquired cheaply.
- **Fail** — incompatible with current constraints or unacceptable risk.

Reject or park a failed candidate. Do not rescue it with a high weighted score.

## 3. Score evidence and attractiveness

Use 0–5 only to support comparison. Attach a confidence grade and note to every score.

### Problem dimensions

- **Evidence strength:** direct behavior, economic commitment, and source independence.
- **Frequency:** how often the triggering situation occurs for the segment.
- **Urgency:** how quickly action is required.
- **Consequence:** time, money, risk, missed opportunity, or emotional load.
- **Substitute cost:** current spending, labor, switching, or tolerated loss.

### Founder and distribution dimensions

- **Founder insight:** depth of situated understanding.
- **Founder access:** ability to reach and learn from target users.
- **Credibility:** permission, trust, or domain legitimacy needed to enter.
- **Acquisition premise:** observable channel with target-user fit.
- **Endurance:** willingness to support the workflow and customer type.

### Product and business dimensions

- **Entry wedge:** narrowness and clarity of the first useful promise.
- **Time to value:** speed from adoption to meaningful progress.
- **Repeat or expansion:** recurring trigger, growing scope, or adjacent value.
- **Payer logic:** identified budget, payment moment, and beneficiary alignment.
- **Operating leverage:** support, service, infrastructure, and gross-margin implications.
- **Accumulating advantage:** data improvement, workflow depth, trust, integration, community, or distribution.

### Feasibility and risk dimensions

- **Time to learn:** how quickly the riskiest assumption can be tested.
- **Build feasibility:** ability to deliver the smallest safe test.
- **Trust and privacy:** required assurance and data exposure.
- **Regulation and safety:** harm if wrong and relevant obligations.
- **Platform dependency:** review, policy, API, ranking, or gatekeeper exposure.
- **Market response:** incumbent reaction and ease of imitation.

### Evidence annotation

Write scores as:

`4 / B — Three recent first-party episodes show weekly manual reconciliation; no observed workflow yet.`

Never write a bare score. Use **U** where no evidence exists.

### Weighting

Choose weights only after stating the founder's objective. A bootstrapped cash-flow project may favor reachability, payment timing, and low operating burden. A learning project may favor time to learn and portfolio relevance. A public-benefit project may favor consequence and access while using different funding logic.

Do not present one weighting scheme as universal.

## 4. Build a portfolio

Prefer three finalists with different risk profiles:

- **Evidence leader:** strongest current problem and behavior evidence.
- **Access leader:** best founder reach, insight, and speed to learn.
- **Option-value outlier:** uncertain but differentiated opportunity with a cheap disconfirmation path.

One candidate may occupy multiple roles. Return fewer than three when the rest fail gates.

For each candidate, show:

- Why it survived.
- What evidence is strongest.
- What could quickly kill it.
- What type of project it is: recurring software, transaction, service-enabled software, productized service, one-time tool, marketplace, or non-commercial utility.
- How it differs from the other finalists.

Avoid a leaderboard that implies false precision. Show tradeoffs and confidence.

## 5. Choose the next learning action

Find the most dangerous assumption, not the easiest metric.

### Customer assumption

Risk: the segment is incorrectly defined or unreachable.

Possible tests: recruit through a named channel, screen for recent behavior, compare two subsegments, or observe a workflow.

### Problem assumption

Risk: the friction is rare, mild, or already solved.

Possible tests: recent-episode interviews, artifact collection with consent, process observation, substitute-spend review, or manual task completion.

### Solution assumption

Risk: the mechanism does not create the promised progress.

Possible tests: paper workflow, concierge delivery, clickable prototype, data sample, or narrow technical spike.

### Distribution assumption

Risk: customers cannot be acquired economically or with available trust.

Possible tests: channel-specific outreach, partner conversation, intent page, community contribution, or targeted content response.

### Retention assumption

Risk: value is one-time or the trigger does not recur.

Possible tests: repeated manual delivery, scheduled follow-up, cohort return behavior, or expansion request.

### Economic assumption

Risk: no aligned payer or insufficient value capture.

Possible tests: paid diagnostic, deposit, letter of intent with terms, paid pilot, preorder, or current budget mapping.

Use the weakest sufficient artifact. Do not build a product when a manual service can expose the assumption more directly.

## 6. Decide pursue, research, park, or reject

### Pursue

Use when gates pass, evidence supports meaningful progress, the founder can reach users, and the next validation is proportionate.

### Research further

Use when one or two material unknowns are cheaply learnable and no integrity or safety gate fails. Specify the evidence required and deadline.

### Park

Use when the opportunity may become attractive after a platform, regulation, founder-access, or technical change, but current learning cost is too high. Name the reactivation trigger.

### Reject

Use when evidence contradicts the problem, access is implausible, economics are structurally unattractive for the objective, risk is unacceptable, or the concept is only cosmetic novelty.

Define before each test:

- **Pass rule:** evidence sufficient to fund the next step.
- **Revise rule:** signal suggests a narrower segment, different problem, or different mechanism.
- **Stop rule:** evidence or cost that ends the current thesis.
- **Budget:** maximum founder hours and cash before review.

## 7. Hand off to marketing

Create `assets/validation-handoff.md` for each selected opportunity. Transfer:

1. Customer, trigger, job, and consequence.
2. Evidence ledger with source and grade.
3. Current substitute and spending.
4. Product promise and mechanism hypothesis.
5. Founder access, credibility, and acquisition premise.
6. Recurring value and payer hypothesis.
7. Hard-gate results and material risks.
8. Riskiest assumption and proposed test.
9. Pass, revise, and stop rules.
10. Open questions and rejected interpretations.

Then use `$indie-product-marketing` for demand validation, channel strategy, launch planning, instrumentation, and growth diagnosis. Discovery has succeeded when the next decision is clearer, even when the correct result is “do not build this.”
