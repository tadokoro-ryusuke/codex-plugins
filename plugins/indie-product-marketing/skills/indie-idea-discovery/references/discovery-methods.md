# Problem Discovery Methods

Use this reference to turn founder context and market traces into concrete problem evidence without leading respondents toward a preferred solution.

## Contents

1. Frame a searchable territory
2. Run the founder inventory
3. Collect behavioral evidence
4. Interview without pitching
5. Mine public and operational traces
6. Normalize and grade evidence
7. Form problem cards
8. Common errors

## 1. Frame a searchable territory

Define a territory narrowly enough to investigate but broadly enough to reveal different solutions:

`actor or community + recurring situation + desired progress + founder boundary`

Examples:

- Independent property managers reconciling repair work with owners, researched by a founder with bookkeeping experience.
- Japanese families coordinating recurring administrative deadlines, researched by a mobile developer with access to parent communities.
- Small engineering teams preparing customer security questionnaires, researched by a developer who has completed them repeatedly.

Avoid territories defined only by a technology such as “AI apps,” a format such as “mobile app,” or a vague demographic such as “tools for everyone.”

## 2. Run the founder inventory

Ask for concrete episodes, not flattering self-descriptions.

### Experience and access

- What work have you performed repeatedly enough to notice exceptions?
- Which customers, professions, communities, or institutions will speak candidly with you?
- What do people already ask you to explain, repair, organize, or recommend?
- Which systems, datasets, workflows, languages, or regulations can you interpret unusually well?

### Behavior and workarounds

- What did you copy between tools in the last seven days?
- What reminders, spreadsheets, scripts, saved prompts, checklists, or private notes have you built?
- Which task causes you to postpone, batch, or ask another person for help?
- When did a missing detail recently create rework, anxiety, an error, or lost money?

### Spending and commitment

- Which subscriptions, contractors, assistants, consultants, or supplies do you pay for?
- Which tool do you dislike but continue paying for?
- Where would switching create real risk or migration cost?
- What have you tried to solve more than once?

### Constraints and motives

- What can you build, support, and sell with the available hours and cash?
- Which customers or operating burdens do you not want?
- Is the objective revenue, learning, portfolio evidence, public benefit, or personal utility?
- What outcome would make this project worth continuing six months from now?

Write answers in `assets/founder-inventory.md`. Mark self-reports as reported facts until supported by behavior or records.

## 3. Collect behavioral evidence

Prioritize traces that show a customer trying to make progress:

| Trace | What it may show | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Repeated manual workflow | Frequency and operational friction | Willingness to adopt a new tool |
| Paid substitute | Budget and valued outcome | Satisfaction or switching intent |
| Spreadsheet or script | Unmet customization need | A broad market |
| Work delegated to a person | Consequence and service economics | Software suitability |
| Support thread | Breakdown in a current solution | Representativeness |
| Review with context | Situation, expected job, and failure | Identity or current truth without checking |
| Job posting | Organizational demand and workflow | Product budget |
| Regulation or deadline | Forced behavior and timing | Customer reachability |
| Search query | Expressed information intent | Problem severity or purchase intent |
| Waitlist sign-up | Message interest and small commitment | Product use or retention |

Look for triangulation across different source types. Ten copied complaints from one thread are one source cluster, not ten independent confirmations.

## 4. Interview without pitching

Investigate recent behavior before discussing an idea.

Ask:

- Tell me about the last time this happened.
- What triggered it?
- What were you trying to finish?
- Walk me through the tools, people, and steps.
- Where did you pause, repeat work, or make an error?
- What happened because of the delay or mistake?
- How often has this occurred recently?
- What have you tried, paid for, or decided not to try?
- Who else is involved, and who owns the budget or risk?
- What would make changing the current process unacceptable?

Avoid:

- “Would you use an app that…?”
- “Is this a good idea?”
- “Would AI make this easier?”
- “How much would you pay?” before understanding current spending and consequence.
- Showing a polished mockup before learning the unaided workflow.

If a concept must be shown, clearly separate the interview into a problem section and a later proposition-response section.

## 5. Mine public and operational traces

Choose sources according to the target customer:

### Consumer and prosumer

- App-store reviews with version and date.
- Community discussions tied to a concrete event.
- Marketplace purchases, templates, courses, and service providers.
- Observable routines, coordination failures, and household workarounds.

### Business and professional

- Product reviews, help centers, status pages, integration directories, and migration guides.
- Job descriptions, procurement documents, implementation partners, and consultant offerings.
- Regulatory guidance, required reports, forms, audit artifacts, and renewal cycles.
- Spreadsheet templates, standard operating procedures, and service-provider pricing.

### Developer tools

- Issue trackers, discussions, documentation gaps, error messages, abandoned repositories, and migration work.
- Usage prerequisites, security constraints, deployment environments, and integration maintenance.
- Paid support, hosted alternatives, sponsorship, and infrastructure spend.

### Creator and marketplace products

- Commission fees, manual fulfillment, sponsorship workflows, rights management, reconciliation, and audience migration.
- Supply acquisition, quality control, trust, moderation, liquidity, and off-platform leakage.

Respect terms, privacy, consent, and community norms. Do not collect or reproduce sensitive personal data simply because it is accessible.

## 6. Normalize and grade evidence

Record each item with:

- Date and source URL or internal record.
- Actor and segment.
- Trigger and intended progress.
- Observed or reported behavior.
- Substitute and cost.
- Consequence.
- Evidence class.
- Independence from other evidence.
- Recency and applicability.
- Alternative explanations.

Use a practical confidence grade:

- **A — direct behavioral/economic evidence:** observed repeated workflow, actual purchase, signed commitment, product analytics, or primary record.
- **B — detailed first-party report:** recent episode with steps, substitute, and consequence, but not independently observed.
- **C — contextual public trace:** credible review, support discussion, market artifact, or institutional document with some missing context.
- **D — weak indication:** generic opinion, engagement, keyword volume, trend claim, or uncited aggregation.
- **U — unknown:** unsupported assumption that must be tested.

Do not average evidence grades. One grade-A signal from one customer can still be narrow; many grade-D signals can still be noise.

## 7. Form problem cards

Create one card per distinct actor–situation–problem combination:

1. **Actor and trigger** — who encounters it, and when?
2. **Desired progress** — what are they trying to complete or avoid?
3. **Current sequence** — tools, people, handoffs, and decisions.
4. **Friction** — delay, uncertainty, coordination, error, cost, risk, or emotional load.
5. **Substitute** — current product, human service, workaround, or non-consumption.
6. **Consequence** — quantified where evidence permits.
7. **Evidence** — source items and grades.
8. **Frequency and variability** — routine, episodic, seasonal, or rare.
9. **Payer and beneficiary** — potentially different people.
10. **Unknowns and disconfirming evidence** — what may make the problem unattractive?

Then write:

> [Actor], when [trigger], tries to [progress] but [friction], so they use [substitute] and incur [consequence].

Keep solution language out of the statement.

## 8. Common errors

- Turning the founder's hobby into a market claim without finding other actors.
- Counting repeated copies of one story as independent demand.
- Treating a feature complaint as proof that users will switch.
- Selecting sources because they support a favorite concept.
- Ignoring people who stopped attempting the task.
- Assuming a painful workflow should be software rather than a service, process change, or education.
- Ignoring the payer, procurement path, or person who bears implementation risk.
- Rejecting a category as “saturated” without finding an underserved segment or trigger.
- Choosing high-frequency but trivial friction over lower-frequency severe consequences without stating the tradeoff.
- Asking an AI to invent quotes, customers, source links, or numerical market evidence.

If evidence remains weak, return a research plan and a narrower territory instead of manufacturing a confident opportunity list.
